GREAT WHITE + BULL + TIGER SHARK VISION × LOUD WETSUITS

LOUD IS A
CONTRAST.

A species-focused brief for designing wetsuits that read as visually conspicuous to the three shark species responsible for most serious human interactions—not merely colorful to humans.

WORKING HYPOTHESISFor both target species, “loud” is most defensibly modeled as species-weighted brightness contrast, bold spatial boundaries, silhouette structure, and motion—not human hue labels.
01

WHAT THE EVIDENCE SAYS

Color is not the whole signal.

01 / GREAT WHITE

Retinal transcriptomics finds one LWS cone opsin, supporting cone monochromacy. Anatomy also shows a cone-rich central retina suited to acute daylight vision. Great whites can be highly visual without human-like color vision.

02 / BULL SHARK

Direct retinal measurements find rods plus a single cone class. Bull-shark cone sensitivity peaks near 554 nm in measured animals, making wavelength-dependent brightness—especially in green coastal light—important.

03 / TIGER SHARK

Retinal transcriptomics finds a single RH2 cone opsin. A high-density band in the ventral retina appears specialized for resolving objects above the shark, making surface silhouette and ventral suit design especially relevant.

04 / LOUD ≠ SAFE

Conspicuous design and deterrence are different questions. A visible suit might aid recognition—or attract attention. Only controlled behavioral testing can distinguish them.

01B

TARGET SPECIES

One concept, three viewing ecologies.

CARCHARODON CARCHARIAS

Great white

Evidence: single LWS cone opsin; cone-rich central retina; strong daylight capability; direct field evidence that counterillumination and silhouette structure alter attacks on seal decoys.

Design emphasis: surf backlighting, ventral brightness, large silhouette-breaking structures, and medium-to-long viewing distances.

CARCHARHINUS LEUCAS

Bull shark

Evidence: rods plus one cone class; measured rod peak near 518 nm and cone peak near 554 nm; approximately 4:1 peak rod-to-cone ratio.

Design emphasis: oversized light-dark blocks that survive green coastal or turbid estuary water. Fine patterns should be assumed to disappear quickly.

GALEOCERDO CUVIER

Tiger shark

Evidence: single RH2 cone opsin; ventral retinal specialization providing higher resolution in the upper visual field.

Design emphasis: conspicuous ventral and side panels for a shark viewing a swimmer from below; retain bold boundaries against complex reef and surface backgrounds.

MODEL STATUS

What differs in Shark View

The selector adjusts approximate wavelength weighting and spatial-detail loss. Bull-shark mode weights the measured longer-wave pigment response and harsher visibility assumptions; great-white and tiger modes reflect their opsin class and viewing ecology.

SHARED LIMIT

No proven color experience

All three currently have evidence for only one cone class. That supports achromatic modeling, but receptor response is not the same as a known subjective experience. The preview is an engineering hypothesis.

SAFETY LIMIT

Visibility is not deterrence

A suit that is easier to detect could reduce mistaken identity, increase inspection, or do neither. Only controlled trials can establish behavioral effects.

02

DESIGN TRANSLATION

Build for the channel.

HIGHEST PRIORITY

Luminance contrast

Test adjacent panels by grayscale value, not hex-code distance. Cyan and pink can look different to us yet collapse into similar brightness underwater.

HIGH PRIORITY

Silhouette breakup

Place sharp light–dark boundaries across limbs and torso edges so the moving human outline fragments at likely viewing distances.

HIGH PRIORITY

Pattern scale

Use large blocks first. Fine checks and stripes may blend into a uniform tone at distance because shark spatial resolution is limited.

TEST CAREFULLY

Motion flicker

Alternating high-contrast panels across joints could create strong temporal signals as arms and legs move. That may be conspicuous without being aversive.

ENVIRONMENTAL

Viewing geometry

Prioritize ventral and side views. A predator approaching from below sees a suit against bright downwelling light—not against a neutral design canvas.

SECONDARY

Human color

Keep the palette loud for identity and delight, but optimize pairs by measured underwater reflectance and shark-weighted luminance.

04

CORE READING

Academic evidence stack.

2025 / MOLECULAR BIOLOGY & EVOLUTION

Great-white and tiger-shark cone monochromacy

Species-specific retinal transcriptomes found one cone opsin in each: LWS in great whites and RH2 in tiger sharks. This is the strongest current basis for their achromatic models.

Hart et al. · PMID 39937658 ↗ 2022 / J EXP MAR BIOL ECOL

Prey interactions in tiger sharks

Accounts for tiger-shark visual perception in animal-borne camera footage and highlights a ventral retinal specialization serving the visual field above the shark.

Ryan et al. · DOI 10.1016/j.jembe.2022.151764 ↗ 2012 / JOURNAL OF MORPHOLOGY

Bull-shark retinal anatomy

Bull sharks possess a duplex retina with rods and single cones and a peak rod-to-cone ratio near 4:1, consistent with substantial visual activity across light conditions.

Schieber, Collin & Hart · DOI 10.1002/jmor.11033 ↗ 2020 / MOLECULAR BIOLOGY & EVOLUTION

Bull-shark spectral sensitivity

Summarizes direct bull-shark pigment measurements: rods near 518 nm and a single cone class near 554 nm, with an A1/A2 chromophore mixture.

Davies et al. · DOI 10.1093/molbev/msz269 ↗ 2024 / CURRENT BIOLOGY

Counterillumination reduces bites by Great White sharks

Field experiments with illuminated seal decoys. Shape, motion, and silhouette contrast affected attacks; brighter-than-background lighting was most effective.

Ryan et al. · DOI 10.1016/j.cub.2024.10.042 ↗ 2011 / NATURWISSENSCHAFTEN

Microspectrophotometric evidence for cone monochromacy in sharks

Measured retinal pigments in 17 shark species. Each species had only one long-wavelength-sensitive cone type.

Hart et al. · DOI 10.1007/s00114-010-0758-8 ↗ 2020 / MOLECULAR BIOLOGY & EVOLUTION

Visual opsin diversity in sharks and rays

Combined opsin sequencing and photoreceptor measurements; found widespread ray dichromacy but confirmed cone monochromacy across studied sharks.

Davies et al. · DOI 10.1093/molbev/msz269 ↗ 2014 / J COMP PHYSIOL A

No rainbow for grey bamboo sharks

Behavioral discrimination experiments controlled brightness and supported the absence of color vision in grey bamboo sharks.

Schluessel et al. · DOI 10.1007/s00359-014-0940-0 ↗ 2012 / BIOLOGY LETTERS

Cone monochromacy and visual pigment tuning in wobbegong sharks

Found only rod RH1 and cone LWS opsins, while showing that spectral tuning still varies with visual ecology.

Theiss et al. · DOI 10.1098/rsbl.2012.0663 ↗ 2015 / AMERICAN NATURALIST

White sharks exploit the sun during predatory approaches

Approach direction changed with sun position, demonstrating that illumination geometry participates in predatory behavior.

Huveneers et al. · DOI 10.1086/680010 ↗ 2014 / ANIMAL COGNITION

Sharks can “see the magic” as well

Grey bamboo sharks perceived illusory contours, evidence that shape completion—not just raw brightness—matters to shark visual processing.

Fuss et al. · PMID 24688458 ↗ 2025 / MOLECULAR BIOLOGY & EVOLUTION

Convergent evolution of cone monochromacy

Retinal transcriptomes across galeomorph sharks indicate cone monochromacy evolved independently multiple times, favoring achromatic vision.

04B

RELATED EXPERIMENTS

What has—and has not—been tested.

Ryan et al. (2024) is unusually direct: it manipulated a visual feature and measured Great White attacks. The studies below establish adjacent mechanisms, but none independently validates a passive patterned wetsuit.

RYAN ET AL. / 2021

Mistaken identity model

Human swimmers, paddling surfers and pinnipeds produced similar shape and motion cues after footage was processed for estimated white-shark acuity. This was a perceptual model, not a live-shark deterrent trial.

DOI 10.1098/rsif.2021.0533 ↗

LAROCHE ET AL. / 2010

Grouped seal decoys

Towed seal-decoy groups showed that white sharks disproportionately attacked individuals with larger domains of danger. It validates controlled decoys as a method, but tested prey spacing rather than pattern.

DOI 10.1098/rsbl.2009.0628 ↗

KOCK / SHAPE CHOICE

Seal versus square

White sharks initially preferred a seal-shaped target to a square target. The work supports prey-shape discrimination and helps explain why the residual dark silhouette in counterillumination trials matters.

University of Pretoria record ↗

MICARELLI ET AL. / 2021–22

Visual prey selection

After odor attracted sharks into the area, white sharks generally selected an odorless seal decoy over tuna bait. Tide, visibility, temperature and sea state changed prey choice—evidence that any visual design must be tested by environment.

2021 study ↗ · environment study ↗

GOIRAN ET AL. / 2022

Sea-snake banding

Predatory reef fish followed solid and banded snake models similarly, but attacks were directed almost entirely at solid-black models. Compelling pattern evidence, but the predators were not our three target sharks.

DOI 10.1098/rspb.2022.1759 ↗

CLAES ET AL. / 2010

Natural counterillumination

Lanternshark bioluminescence provides a biological precedent for reducing a ventral silhouette. It supports the mechanism behind active illumination but does not test attacks on artificial prey or people.

DOI 10.1016/j.jembe.2010.03.012 ↗

DAN / ALERT DIVER / 2024

Shark-deterrent field overview

Divers Alert Network surveys visual, acoustic, chemical, electrical, magnetic and protective approaches. Its visual section documents blue disruptive suits and black-white sea-snake/pilot-fish mimicry, while warning that responses are species-dependent. Particularly relevant: tiger sharks prey on sea snakes, so banding cannot be assumed to warn all target species.

The article also stresses that no deterrent is completely effective and that perceived protection must not encourage riskier behavior. Treat this as a safety-industry overview—not primary experimental validation.

04C

THE OPEN QUESTION

A publishable three-species trial.

No independent peer-reviewed study located in this review directly compares passive wetsuit patterns using controlled moving decoys across Great White, bull and tiger sharks.

  1. 01
    STANDARDIZE THE TARGET

    Use identical decoy geometry, speed, depth and motion. Change only the visual treatment: solid black, sea-snake bands, disruptive patches, bright ventral fields, fluorescent contrast and active counterillumination.

  2. 02
    SEPARATE THE BEHAVIORS

    Record first detection, orientation, follow, close approach, aborted approach, contact and strike. “Attraction” and “attack” must not be treated as the same outcome.

  3. 03
    MEASURE THE SCENE

    Capture material reflectance, underwater irradiance, turbidity, depth, distance, viewing angle, sea state and motion. Reconstruct each target in a species-weighted luminance model.

  4. 04
    REPLICATE BY SPECIES

    Run separate preregistered protocols for Great White, bull and tiger sharks. Do not transfer a positive white-shark result to coastal bull sharks or tiger sharks without direct evidence.

05

RESEARCH ROADMAP

From loud idea to defensible claim.

  1. 01
    SPECTRAL MATERIAL LIBRARY

    Measure neoprene and print reflectance wet and dry. Convert spectra into species-specific cone/rod catches under coastal light fields.

  2. 02
    SHARK-VIEW RENDERER

    Add a luminance-first preview to the designer: depth, water type, viewing angle, distance, blur/acuty, and motion-state controls.

  3. 03
    SPATIAL PATTERN SCREEN

    Compare large blocks, limb bands, asymmetric panels, fine patterns, and silhouette-breaking layouts at multiple simulated distances.

  4. 04
    BEHAVIORAL VALIDATION

    Begin with non-feeding captive choice or detection tasks, then ethically designed field trials using decoys—not people—as stimuli.

OUTREACH POSITIONING

Propose a small, publishable feasibility study—not a request to endorse a product. Lead with measured materials, preregistered hypotheses, animal welfare, and a clear distinction between conspicuity and safety.

IMPORTANT LIMITS

This is a design research program, not a safety claim.

Shark species differ. Lab results do not automatically generalize to open water. A visually conspicuous wetsuit has not been shown here to prevent bites, and could alter behavior in unintended ways. No marketing claim should imply deterrence until independent, species-relevant behavioral evidence exists.
